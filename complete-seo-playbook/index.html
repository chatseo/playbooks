SEO isn't a checklist. It's a chain.

Which is why doing more of it rarely works, and why most audits are worthless.

A checklist implies the items are independent and additive: fix eleven things, get eleven improvements. That is not how any of this behaves. The five parts of the model in the diagram above are multiplied. A perfect page that Google cannot crawl earns nothing. A page that ranks first for a query nobody buys from earns nothing. A page that ranks, converts, and answers the wrong intent gets a click and a back button.

Every part can be at 90% and one part at 10%, and the site performs at 10%. The whole discipline is finding out which part is at 10% before you spend a month on the ones already at 90%.

That is also the reason SEO advice sounds contradictory. "Content is king" and "links are everything" and "technical first" are all correct — each one was written by somebody whose weakest link was that part. None of them knows which yours is.

So the ten chapters below run in the order the work has to happen: find the constraint, pick the queries, find the pages that are close, rank them, match the intent, write the page, point every surface at it, respond to what Google does next, measure the right numbers, and build the whole loop in thirty days.

Read it in order once. Then work it out of order forever — you'll only ever be in one chapter at a time, and the chapter you're in is decided by your weakest link, not by the calendar.

1 · The SEO model

Five parts decide whether SEO works. In this order, because each one gates the next.

The value of a model is not that it is clever. It is that it stops the argument. When five people disagree about what to do next, they are almost always disagreeing about which part is broken — and that is a question with an answer in the data.

Crawlability — can Google reach it, and did it keep it

Not "is my site fast". Indexed versus submitted. If Google has 400 of your 1,200 pages, every other conversation is premature, because two thirds of your site is not in the race at all. The common causes are boring: a noindex left on from staging, a canonical pointing everything at the homepage, parameters generating infinite variants, a robots.txt rule someone added in 2021.

The number: indexed pages ÷ pages you actually want indexed. Under 90% and this is your chapter.

Intent — is this the kind of page the query wants

The most expensive mistake in SEO, and the least visible, because the page looks fine. You wrote a product page for a query where every result in the top ten is a comparison article. Google is not withholding your ranking; it has already decided what shape of answer this query gets, and you brought the wrong shape.

The number: your CTR against the average CTR of your own pages at the same position. A page that ranks well and gets ignored is an intent problem wearing a ranking costume.

Content — is there a reason for this page to exist

Not length. Not keyword coverage. A reason. Something on the page that the five results above it do not have: a number you measured, a screenshot of the actual thing, a step somebody skipped, an opinion with a consequence.

The number: position for the query the page was built for, after 8 weeks of being indexed. Stuck at 20+ with the right intent and a crawlable page means the page did not earn it.

Authority — does anything vouch for this

The part everyone over-indexes on, and the only one you cannot fully control. It matters most in competitive commercial SERPs and least in long-tail informational ones, which is exactly why the "just get links" advice fails so often: it is prescribed to sites whose constraint was one of the other four.

The number: how long your last comparable page took to reach page one. If your pages get there in six weeks, authority is not your constraint. If they never get there, it might be.

Conversion — does the click turn into anything

SEO that ends at the click is a hobby. This is the part that decides whether the previous four were worth doing, and the part almost no SEO report contains.

The number: qualified clicks — clicks from queries a buyer would type — divided by signups or enquiries. If that ratio is near zero, more traffic makes the problem bigger, not smaller.

2 · Query selection

Three filters. A query has to survive all three, and volume is not one of them.

Keyword research goes wrong at the first step, because the tool sorts by search volume and volume is the one attribute that has no relationship to whether the query is worth your month. A query with 40 searches a month from people holding a credit card beats one with 12,000 searches from students writing an essay, every time, and no tool will ever tell you which is which. That judgement is yours.

Filter 1 — would this person ever pay you

Read the query out loud and ask what the person typing it is trying to finish. Three answers exist: they are trying to buy, trying to learn, or trying to get somewhere. Only the first two are yours, and only the first one pays this quarter.

The tell is in the modifiers. best, alternative, vs, pricing, for [industry], software, tool, agency, near me — someone is shopping. what is, why, how does, meaning, examples — someone is learning, which is worth real money but on a longer horizon and only if the page has a next step. Nothing at all, just a bare noun, is usually ambiguous, and ambiguous queries are where budgets go to die.

Filter 2 — can you realistically be there

Not "what is the difficulty score". Those are a single number invented from backlink counts, and they cannot see the two things that actually decide it: whether you already have a foothold, and what type of page Google has chosen for this query.

  • Do you already rank anywhere? A query where you sit at 14 is a different project from one where you do not appear at all. The first is a week; the second is a quarter.
  • What type of page wins? Open the SERP. If the top five are all listicles from publishers and you sell software, your product page will never rank there no matter what you write. You either produce that page type or you pick a different query.
  • Who owns it? If every result is a site a thousand times your size and the query is commercial, that is a two-year fight. Skip it. There is always a more specific version of the same query with the same buyer behind it.

Filter 3 — is it worth the hours

The arithmetic nobody does. Expected clicks at position 3 × your close rate × what a customer is worth, against the hours the page costs. Do it once and half your keyword list disappears, which is the point.

Use your click-through rate at position 3, from your own Search Console, not an industry curve from a blog post. Yours is different, and on branded or niche SERPs it is very different.

3 · Opportunity signals

Finding the pages that are one push from moving — and refusing the metrics that only look like insight.

Most sites are sitting on a month of easy gains and cannot see them, because the gains are hidden inside averages. A site-wide average position of 18 tells you nothing: it is one number covering a page at 3 and a page at 60, and moving it requires moving everything. The signals below are the opposite — each one names specific URLs with a specific reason they are stuck.

And the metrics that are vanity wearing a suit

  • An SEO score out of 100. It is a weighted average of checks a crawler can automate. Nothing in it knows what your pages are for.
  • Domain authority. A third-party model of a Google signal, not the signal. Useful for a rough comparison, useless as a target — you cannot ship a DA increase.
  • Total keywords ranked. Goes up when you publish anything at all, including pages ranking at 87 for phrases nobody types.
  • Site-wide average position. Hides everything that matters, and moves for reasons you did not cause.
  • Impressions on their own. A page can gain 10,000 impressions and zero clicks by ranking 40th for something popular. That is not progress; that is noise with a chart.

The test for any metric: if it moved, would you know what to do differently on Monday? If not, it is a number for a report, not for you.

4 · Page prioritisation

A scorecard, so the argument becomes arithmetic.

The reason SEO backlogs rot is that every item on them looks equally plausible when written as a sentence. "Improve the pricing page" and "fix the blog's internal linking" are the same size on a list and wildly different in expected value. Scoring is not bureaucracy; it is the only thing that makes the list shrink.

Five axes. Four go on top, one goes underneath, because effort is a divisor, not a penalty.

5 · Intent matching

Connecting what you sell to what the query actually wants — without keyword density or a word-count target.

Both of those ideas survive because they are measurable, and being measurable is not the same as being causal. No search engine has ranked on keyword density in over a decade, and "2,400 words" is a description of what already ranked, not an instruction for what will. Copying the length of the winner is cargo cult: you observed the plane, you built the runway, nothing landed.

What actually has to match is the shape of the answer. Google has already decided, for every query, what kind of page satisfies it — and it publishes that decision in the top ten. Your job is to read the decision and then be the best version of it, not to argue with it.

Query shapeWhat they actually wantThe page that winsThe next step that fits
what is X / how does X workTo understand, in one sittingA definition in the first 40 words, then depthA link to the comparison page. Never a demo request.
how to do XTo finish a task todayNumbered steps, screenshots, the failure casesThe tool that does step 4 for them
best X / top X toolsA shortlist someone else madeA list page with real criteria, including where you loseYour entry, honestly placed

The bridge is the part people skip

An informational page with no defined next step is a page that ranks for somebody else's benefit. Not a banner — a next step that belongs to the same thought: the "how to do X manually" page ends where the manual method gets painful, and that is exactly where the product goes. If you cannot name the sentence where a reader would want help, the page probably has no commercial reason to exist, and that is fine — just be aware you have written a public service rather than an asset.

How to check a page in ninety seconds

  • Search the query in an incognito window. Not your keyword tool. The live SERP.
  • Name the format of the top five. If four of five are guides and you have a product page, stop; that is your whole answer.
  • Read your own title as a stranger. Does it promise the thing the query asked for, in the words the query used?
  • Look at your first screen. Is the answer there, or is there a 200-word introduction about why the topic matters?
  • Find the next step. If a satisfied reader has nowhere to go, add one, and make it the thing they'd want next — not the thing you want next.

6 · Content creation

The research method, the page structure, and the linking logic — for pages built around a real reason to rank.

Research: read the SERP, not the tool

Open the top five results and answer three questions, in writing, before drafting anything. What do they all cover — that is the table stakes, and leaving any of it out gets you dismissed. What format did Google choose — that is not negotiable. And what does none of them answer — that is the only reason a sixth page gets to exist.

That third question is the entire job. If you cannot answer it, do not write the page. You will spend six hours producing the sixth-best version of something and it will rank exactly there.

Structure: answer first, evidence second, depth third

The inverted pyramid, which journalism worked out a century ago and SEO keeps rediscovering. The direct answer in the first 40 words serves three readers at once: the person who wanted only that, the featured snippet, and the AI assistant looking for something extractable. Then the evidence that makes the answer believable. Then the depth for the reader who is still there — and they are the one who converts.

  • H1 = the promise, in the query's words. Not a clever variation of it.
  • First 40 words = the answer. If the query has no single answer, say what it depends on, in one sentence.
  • H2s are questions a reader has. If an H2 is a keyword phrase rather than a question, you wrote it for a crawler that stopped caring in 2015.
  • Every claim carries its evidence in the same paragraph. A number, a screenshot, a source, an example. Claims that travel alone are the ones AI answers refuse to cite.
  • One next step at the end. Not four.

Internal linking: the part that compounds

Internal links are the only ranking factor you fully control, and most sites treat them as navigation. The logic is simple and worth being strict about: a hub page covers the topic broadly and links to every spoke; each spoke links back to the hub and sideways to two or three siblings. Anchor text is the query the target page is for, written naturally — not "click here", and not the exact phrase eleven times.

Three in, three out is the working rule. A new page with no internal links pointing at it is a page you published for nobody: Google finds it late, ranks it slowly, and you conclude that content does not work.

7 · Multi-surface execution

Technical, content, links and AI search are four surfaces of one job — not four projects with four owners.

The default failure is organisational rather than technical. Technical works through a crawl report. Content works through a calendar. Links work through outreach targets. And each one optimises its own metric on a different part of the site, so a quarter of effort produces four small unrelated gains and no ranking. The fix is not more coordination meetings. It is pointing all four at one cluster at a time.

Technical serves the cluster

Crawl budget is finite and mostly spent on rubbish: tag archives, parameter variants, paginated listings, old campaign pages. Technical work in a given six weeks means making sure the cluster you are building is crawled quickly and everything competing with it for attention is not. That is a much smaller job than "fix all 200 issues", and it is the half that actually affects the ranking you are chasing.

Links serve the pages that are close

The single most wasted link in SEO points at a homepage that already ranks for its own brand. Authority flows: point external links, and every internal link you control, at the page sitting at position 11 for a query worth money. A page at 11 with three good links moves. A homepage with three good links does nothing you can measure.

AI search is a citation problem, not a keyword problem

Assistants do not rank ten blue links; they assemble an answer and cite a handful of sources. What gets cited is what can be lifted cleanly: a claim stated in one sentence, a number with its source attached, a definition near the top of the page, a table that survives being read aloud. The practical consequence is that the structure from chapter 6 — answer first, evidence in the same paragraph — is not just good for readers. It is the format that makes a page quotable, and quotable is the new ranked.

Two things follow. Vague claims stop earning anything at all, because there is nothing to quote. And brand queries matter more than they did, because an assistant that mentions you creates the search that brings a buyer to your site directly.

Content is the only surface that creates the asset

The other three amplify. If the page does not deserve to rank, technical makes it crawlable faster, links make it authoritative at 30, and AI search skips it for something quotable. The order is not negotiable: build the thing, then aim the other three at it.

The working rule: one cluster, six weeks, all four surfaces. Then move. Four surfaces running independently on four parts of the site is the most common way to spend a year on SEO and have nothing to show for it.

8 · Ranking response handling

The costly mistake here is not misreading a drop. It is acting on a drop that was never a ranking problem — rewriting a page that held position perfectly while an AI answer appeared above it. The first question is never "how do I fix this". It is "which of these six things happened".

   above you. This is not a ranking problem.
3. POSITION HELD, CTR held, impressions fell — demand fell. Nothing is broken. Say so and stop.
4. TWO URLS, one query — cannibalisation. Name both and say which should keep it.
5. IMPRESSIONS STOPPED — an indexing problem, not a ranking one. Say what to check first.
6. FLAT SINCE PUBLICATION, 8+ weeks — the page never entered. This is not a drop.

Name the week the change started, taken from the weekly series, not from an update you
remember. If you think a Google update is involved, ask me for the date rather than
asserting one.

Do not offer more than one pattern. If the data supports two, say which is more likely and
why, in one sentence.
If a figure is not in the data, write "cannot compute from this data".

End with ONE action, and the date I should check whether it worked.

9 · Measurement

Eight numbers, in a chain. Where the chain breaks is what you work on next month.

Measurement in SEO usually fails in one of two directions: a dashboard with forty widgets nobody reads, or a single traffic number that cannot tell you why it moved. The way out is to measure the chain rather than the total, because a chain localises the fault.

Template 8 — the weekly dashboard

Eight rows. If a number is flat, write flat. A week where nothing happened is a valid report and should take ninety seconds to read.

#The numberIf it's the one that's broken
1Indexed pages vs pages you want indexedCrawlability. Nothing below this matters yet.
2Impressions, this week vs lastDemand or coverage. Check whether you lost queries or the world stopped asking.
3Queries in positions 1–3 / 4–10 / 11–20The only position metric worth keeping. Movement between bands is the real signal.
4CTR by position bandPackaging. You are ranking and not being chosen.
5ClicksThe output of 1–4. Never the thing you diagnose from.
6Qualified clicks — from queries a buyer would typeQuery selection. You are winning the wrong searches.
7Signups or enquiries from organicConversion, or intent match on the landing page.
8Revenue from organicThe only number that ends the argument about whether any of this worked.

Row 6 is the one almost nobody tracks and the one that changes behaviour fastest. A month where clicks rose 30% and qualified clicks were flat is a month you got more popular with people who will never buy — useful to know in week two rather than in quarter three.

Three numbers to delete from your reporting

  • Average position across the site. Replaced by row 3. One number covering a page at 3 and a page at 60 is not a measurement, it's an average of two unrelated things.
  • Total keywords ranked. Rises when you publish anything. Falls when you delete dead pages, which is usually a good week.
  • Any third-party authority score. It is a model of a signal, updated on someone else's schedule. You cannot ship a change to it.

The cadence that survives contact with a real week

Weekly: the eight numbers, ten minutes, one action. Monthly: the opportunity scan and the scorecard, forty-five minutes, one page ships. Quarterly: back to chapter 1 — has the constraint moved? It usually has, and continuing to work last quarter's constraint is how a good year turns into a flat one.

10 · The 30-day build

Four weeks. Each one ends in something that exists, not something you understand.

Week 1 — fix the technical base

Not a full audit. Four checks, in order, stopping at the first thing that is broken: is everything you want indexed actually indexed; is anything indexed twice; are template pages (tags, author archives, internal search) eating the crawl; and are your commercial pages receiving impressions for the queries they were written for. Page speed, alt text and heading order are not on this list, and that is deliberate — none of them has ever been the reason a site with an indexing gap was not ranking.

Week 2 — map the opportunities

Query worksheet on twelve candidates, opportunity scan on what you already have, scorecard over both. The week ends with one page named and a check date written down. It does not end with a content calendar — a calendar is a promise about weeks you have not lived yet, and it will be wrong by week three.

Week 3 — ship one cluster

Either one hub and three spokes if you are building new, or five existing pages fixed if the scan found striking-distance pages, which it usually does. Fixing five pages that already have impressions beats publishing four new ones almost every time in month one, because the feedback arrives in weeks rather than months and you need the feedback more than you need the pages.

Internal links go in as you ship, not afterwards. "I'll do the linking later" is the single most common unfinished task in SEO.

Week 4 — install the learning loop

Every change you shipped gets a check date and a success metric. The eight-number dashboard gets built once. The response matrix gets saved where you will actually find it when something moves. This is the week with nothing to show for it, which is exactly why it gets dropped — and dropping it means month two starts from the same blank page as month one.

If you only get two weeks: do week 1 and week 3. A sound base and five fixed pages beats a perfect plan you didn't ship.

The five mistakes that kill this

Working the interesting part instead of the broken one

Content is the fun chapter. It is also the wrong chapter if two thirds of your pages aren't indexed. The model exists to overrule your preference, and it will overrule it in a direction you don't like at least once.

Sorting by search volume

Volume is the one attribute in a keyword tool with no relationship to whether the query is worth your month. It survives because it's the only column that's always populated.

Publishing before the reason exists

If you can't name the thing none of the top five say, you are about to spend six hours building the sixth-best page on the topic, and it will rank exactly there.

Reacting to a drop that wasn't one

Position held, CTR fell means the SERP changed, not your page. Rewriting the body of a page that is still ranking is how a bad week becomes a bad quarter.

Skipping the check date

"I shipped it" and "it worked" are different claims. Without a date and a metric written before you ship, you accumulate changes and learn nothing — which is indistinguishable from doing no SEO at all, only more expensive.

Where this still needs a human

  • Whether the buyer is real. Filter 1 in chapter 2 is a judgement about your market. No dataset contains it.
  • Whether the page can honestly deliver the query. You can write the title that earns the click. Only you know if the page behind it keeps the promise, and a click you don't keep is worse than one you never got.
  • What's worth building. The scorecard ranks what exists. The speculative cluster that scores last might still be the right bet — that's conviction, not arithmetic.
  • The weird stuff. If a number looks wrong, it might be. Go and look at the page.

What a playbook structurally cannot do

Worth being straight about, because you'll hit it in week five and it isn't a flaw in the method.

Everything above is a method, and a method has no eyes and no memory.

Every loop on this page depends on someone noticing. The scorecard needs this month's numbers, exported by hand. The response matrix only fires if somebody spots the movement — and decay never announces itself: a page loses four clicks a week for six months, no alert fires, and by the time it's visible in the monthly total it's a rebuild rather than a fix. The check dates you wrote in week 4 have to be checked by a person who remembers writing them.

That's why week 4 is the week everyone drops. It's the only week whose output is a habit rather than a page, and habits are exactly what a printed method can't supply.
